\section{Adversarial Payload Generation as Constrained Optimization}
\label{sec:opt}

Hand-written payloads such as those in Fig.~\ref{fig:running-example} are guesses. This section formulates the search for an effective payload as an optimization problem, which allows payloads to be generated systematically and adapted to a specific model and field.

\subsection{Problem formulation}
\label{sec:opt-problem}

Let $f$ be the target field, with admissible set
\begin{equation}
\adm(f) = \{\, \delta \in \Sigma_f^{*} \;:\; |\delta| \le L_f \,\},
\label{eq:admissible}
\end{equation}
let $\ctx(\delta)$ be the copilot's context with payload $\delta$ inserted into field $f$, and let $y^{*}$ be the attacker's target output (e.g.\ the label \texttt{BENIGN}).

\begin{Definition}[Constrained payload optimization]
\label{def:opt}
\begin{equation}
\delta^{*} = \arg\max_{\delta \in \adm(f)} \; \underbrace{\log p_\theta\big(y^{*} \mid \ctx(\delta)\big)}_{\text{fitness } F(\delta)} \;-\; \lambda\, D(\delta),
\label{eq:opt}
\end{equation}
where $D(\delta) \ge 0$ is an optional detectability penalty and $\lambda \ge 0$ its weight.
\end{Definition}

The search space is discrete and enormous: it contains up to $|\Sigma_f|^{L_f}$ strings, about $95^{200}$ for a 200-character User-Agent restricted to printable ASCII. Exhaustive search is impossible, and because strings are discrete, the gradient methods of continuous optimization cannot be applied directly. The field length $L_f$ is the main difficulty parameter: a 200-character User-Agent leaves room for a full natural-language instruction, whereas a 32-character username leaves room for only a few words, so the attacker must find unusually effective short strings.

Using the log-probability of $y^{*}$ as fitness, rather than a binary success flag, is essential. Most candidate payloads fail, and a binary flag gives every failure the same score, providing no direction for the search. A continuous fitness distinguishes a payload that raises $p_\theta(\texttt{BENIGN})$ from $0.01$ to $0.4$ from one that leaves it unchanged, even though both still fail.

\subsection{Gradient-guided search (GCG)}
\label{sec:opt-gcg}

Greedy Coordinate Gradient~\cite{zou2023gcg} operates on the token sequence of the payload. Representing token $i$ by a one-hot vector $e_{x_i} \in \{0,1\}^{|\vocab|}$, it computes the gradient of the loss $\mathcal{L}(\delta) = -\log p_\theta(y^{*}\mid \ctx(\delta))$ with respect to that vector, and uses its most negative entries as candidate substitutions:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{X}_i = \operatorname{top-}k\big(-\nabla_{e_{x_i}} \mathcal{L}(\delta)\big).
\label{eq:gcg}
\end{equation}
At every iteration a batch of single-token substitutions is sampled from the sets $\mathcal{X}_i$, each candidate is evaluated exactly, and the best one is kept.

GCG has three limitations in the log setting. First, it requires gradients, i.e.\ white-box access. Second, it typically optimizes a suffix of tens of tokens, which a short field such as a username cannot hold. Third, the substituted tokens must decode to characters in $\Sigma_f$ (\S\ref{sec:llm-ar}), so candidate sets $\mathcal{X}_i$ must be filtered, which reduces the effective search space and can slow convergence.

\subsection{Evolutionary search (genetic algorithm)}
\label{sec:opt-ga}

A genetic algorithm (GA)~\cite{holland1992adaptation} needs only the fitness value $F(\delta)$, i.e.\ grey-box access. Individuals are candidate payloads; Algorithm~\ref{alg:ga} gives the general scheme.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Genetic algorithm for payload search}
\label{alg:ga}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE field $f$, population size $N$, generations $G$, fitness $F$
\STATE $P \leftarrow$ $N$ seed payloads in $\adm(f)$ (e.g.\ hand-written)
\FOR{$g = 1$ to $G$}
  \STATE evaluate $F(\delta)$ for every $\delta \in P$
  \STATE $E \leftarrow$ best individuals of $P$ (elitism)
  \WHILE{$|E| < N$}
    \STATE select parents $\delta_a,\delta_b$ from $P$ (tournament)
    \STATE $\delta' \leftarrow \mathrm{mutate}(\mathrm{crossover}(\delta_a,\delta_b))$
    \STATE $\delta' \leftarrow \mathrm{repair}_f(\delta')$ \COMMENT{enforce $\delta' \in \adm(f)$}
    \STATE $E \leftarrow E \cup \{\delta'\}$
  \ENDWHILE
  \STATE $P \leftarrow E$
\ENDFOR
\RETURN $\arg\max_{\delta\in P} F(\delta)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The operators act directly on strings. \emph{Mutation} inserts, deletes, or substitutes a unit of the payload, where the unit can be a character, a token, or a whole word (word-level mutation preserves readability). \emph{One-point crossover} cuts both parents at a random position and joins the prefix of one with the suffix of the other. Since both operators can produce invalid strings, a \emph{repair} step truncates the child to $L_f$ and removes or maps characters outside $\Sigma_f$, so that every individual stays in $\adm(f)$. Other population-based methods are less suitable: particle swarm optimization (PSO), for example, is defined over continuous vector spaces and requires a non-trivial discrete adaptation to operate on strings.

\subsection{LLM-driven iterative refinement (PAIR)}
\label{sec:opt-pair}

PAIR~\cite{chao2025pair} uses a second \emph{attacker LLM} that proposes a payload, observes the target's response, and rewrites the payload in light of the failure, repeating until success or a query budget is exhausted. It requires only black-box access.

PAIR can be viewed as a search method in which the role of the gradient is played by natural-language feedback: instead of a numerical direction, the attacker LLM receives the target's response and reasons about how to change the payload. Its payloads are therefore fluent, human-readable sentences, which are harder to filter than the unusual token sequences produced by GCG (whose high perplexity can be detected). In return, PAIR has no direct mechanism for respecting hard constraints: the length limit and character set can only be requested in the attacker's prompt, and violating candidates must be rejected or repaired afterwards.

\subsection{Transferability}
\label{sec:opt-transfer}

A payload is \emph{transferable} if, optimized against a source model $\theta_s$, it also succeeds against a target model $\theta_t \ne \theta_s$. Transferability is what makes white-box optimization dangerous in practice: an attacker can optimize against a downloadable open-weight model and then attack a deployment they can only observe as a black box~\cite{zou2023gcg}.
